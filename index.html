he'll actually install

When a CSP won't install a booking, he tells us why he's declining — and today we throw that signal away and re-offer blindly, often to the wrong CSP again. This turns declines into a self-learning loop: capture the reason in context, help the CSP avoid a careless decline, verify it's real, and let the right service act on it. We start with the single biggest reason of all. The objective — filter the clutter, so every CSP gets only quality, serviceable bookings.

Context · what a decline is

When a CSP won't install, he tells us why

A booking sent to a CSP can be turned down at two moments — a decline before he accepts, or an install-failure report after — and at both he picks a reason from the same fixed list. That reason is the signal this whole exercise is built on. First, the lay of the land.

Where we start

One reason dwarfs the rest — "Service not available at this location", at ~65% of all declines. So we build the full self-learning loop for that one reason first, end-to-end. The others follow the same five-step spine later (kept in the archive). The rest of this document is that one reason.

The loop in one picture · service not available

Five steps, one reason, all the way around

Here's the loop for that one reason. It only counts as built if it runs end-to-end: from the CSP's tap to Genie actually dropping him from the candidate list for that point.

Step 1 · Show

Reason in context

Surface the reason at the right moment, framed as neutral signal.

Step 2 · Assist & capture

Informed decline

Show him the nearby points, let him decide, and capture the ones he still rejects.

Step 3 · Verify

Is it a false decline?

Test the claim against our own data and aligned-incentive ground truth.

Step 4 · Signal

Emit to Genie

Hand the verified signal to serviceability. We don't act — we inform.

Step 5 · Consequence

Genie drops him from candidates

Past a threshold, Genie stops surfacing him for bookings near that point, so DAS never routes them to him.

↻The self-learning loop: once Genie drops that active point, the CSP stops appearing as a candidate for bookings there — so DAS never sends him those, and the clutter he used to decline never arrives again. Fewer bad offers → fewer coverage declines → a cleaner supply map every month.
Why we're doing this

The objective

For this reason, the goal is to send only quality serviceable bookings to the CSP and filter out the clutter — automatically, by learning from every decline. (Success metrics are at the end of the walkthrough below.)

How this helps the customer — the answer to "what happens after Step 5"

A customer in a genuinely-serviced area stops being sent to a CSP who'll refuse them. Their booking lands on a CSP who will install — first time — instead of bouncing through re-offers until it times out. The loop's payoff is not a cleaner dashboard; it's a customer who waits days less to get online.

Service not available · end to end

The one reason, through all five steps

1
Show

Show the reason list this layer

Step 1 does one thing: show the CSP the list of decline reasons and let him pick the one that fits. What matters here is which reasons are visible, that they read as neutral options, and that their order is randomised on every render so a reason isn't chosen just because it sits on top. The CSP taps the option matching his situation — for this exercise, that's "service not available at this location". Nothing is interpreted or acted on yet; that starts at Step 2.

More about it →

The full reason-set design — the complete list, and which reasons to keep, remove, club or add — sits in the archive. Scope 1 only needs the list shown correctly.

2
Assist & capture

Help him avoid a false decline — and capture the points he's rejecting this layer

Before we accept the decline, we show the CSP what we already know: he has active connections a few doors away. If he backs off, a false decline never happens. If he still declines, we capture two things — the decision (a deliberate "I won't serve here"), and the specific active or splitter points we just showed him that he's declaring un-serviceable. Those points, not just the booking, are the unit we learn on — and only a point we actually showed him can ever be flagged, never one he never saw.

⚠︎ Are you sure service isn't available here?

● House 42, same lane 40 m
● Green Apt, Block B 95 m
● Shop 7, main road 150 m

What we record

"Let me re-check" → decline avoided. The booking keeps its best CSP. Nothing else happens.

"Still not serviceable" → a deliberate decline, made despite seeing live neighbours. We log it against the points we showed him as one flag: "this CSP won't serve this point."

Showing the neighbours is what converts a careless tap into a high-confidence claim. It's the cheapest false-decline filter we have — and it happens before Step 3 ever runs.

3
Verify

Verify the claim — Ops calling TBD

We never act on a single tap. But note: whatever data we hold about this location has already been shown to the CSP in the Step 2 popup — so re-checking our own data here adds nothing. The one thing data can't settle is his actual claim: that the connection is genuinely too far for him to serve. That needs a human check.

Ops calling

Ops calls to validate "it's too far"

Ops follows up with the CSP to confirm the connection is really beyond his reach, as he claims — not that he simply didn't want it. Only a validated "genuinely far" decline accumulates toward the consequence in Step 5.

TBD

The ops-calling flow — who calls, when, and how a validated "too far" decline is recorded — still needs to be worked out. A verification layer matters more for intent reasons like "customer said no"; for coverage, ops calling is the one check our own data can't make.

4
Signal

Emit the verified signal to Genie Genie acts

This is the scope boundary. We build no candidate list, route nothing, and de-list no one ourselves. We publish a clean, verified, point-level signal — "CSP X, ≥N validated not-serviceable flags for point P" — and Genie reads it. We are the trusted system-of-record; Genie owns the decision.

How the CSP system works: Genie builds each booking's candidate-CSP list by distance from every CSP's active connection points; DAS then routes among those candidates. Neither is us — we only feed Genie the signal that changes which active points count.

5
Consequence

Genie drops the point for that CSP Genie acts

Using the signal, Genie applies a threshold: once a CSP's point (active or splitter point) crosses the flag bar, Genie stops counting that point in the distance calc for this CSP. From then on he isn't surfaced as a candidate for new bookings near that point — so DAS never routes them to him, because we've learned he won't install there. The clutter is gone from his feed, and the booking goes to a CSP who will.

Reason "Customer refused" — customer-side verification loop ›

When a CSP says the customer refused, today we don't validate it — we just re-offer to the next CSP, breaking their experience if the first CSP was right. The same spine applies, but the verification and the action live on the customer side, not here: this layer would only emit a "customer-refused, needs verification" signal, and the customer-side system owns the contact and the outcome.

Customer: "Yes, I don't want it"
▣ Cancel & stop routing

CSP was right. Customer-side cancels + fast-refunds; no other CSP wastes a visit.

Customer: "No, I do want it"
▶ Keep routing + record

Booking stays alive. The disproven claim is recorded for Quality to evaluate — never surfaced to the CSP as a strike.

Customer: no response
◷ As usual

No new information — fall back to today's behaviour. We lose nothing.
